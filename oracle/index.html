    易经

    What is the I Ching?

    The I Ching, or Book of Changes, is a Chinese classic about 3,000 years old. It describes 64 situations, called hexagrams. Each is a stack of six lines, and each line is either yang (solid: strong, active) or yin (broken: yielding, receptive).

    People have long used it as a mirror: you hold a question in mind, cast a hexagram, and read it as a lens on your situation. It doesn't predict the future. It gives you a new angle to think from.

    How the coin toss works

    Each toss of three coins builds one line, from the bottom up. Heads count as 3 and tails as 2, so a toss adds up to 6, 7, 8 or 9:

    • 7 (one head): a solid yang line
    • 8 (two heads): a broken yin line
    • 9 (three heads): yang so strong it's turning to yin, marked ○
    • 6 (three tails): yin so deep it's turning to yang, marked ✕

    Six tosses give you six lines and your hexagram. The 9s and 6s are the rare ones (1 toss in 8 each). They are changing lines, and they show where your situation is in motion.

    读卦

    How to read your hexagram

    The lines

    Lines are read from the bottom up, like a plant growing. Solid black lines are yang, broken black lines are yin. A red line is a changing line: a 9 (three heads, marked ○) or a 6 (three tails, marked ✕) that has reached its extreme and is about to flip.

    Present and Becoming

    Your six lines form the Present hexagram: the situation as it stands. If any lines are changing, flip each one (yang to yin, yin to yang) and you get a second hexagram, Becoming: where things are heading. With no changing lines, the situation is settled and one hexagram speaks on its own.

    The changing lines

    Each changing line points to the part of the situation that's moving. Position matters: line 1 is the beginning, the first stirrings; line 2 is your everyday, private ground; line 3 is the threshold between inner and outer; line 4 is where you meet the wider world; line 5 is the seat of influence, where your own choices weigh most; and line 6 is the summit, where things complete and tip into what comes next.

    The two trigrams

    Every hexagram is two three-line trigrams stacked: the lower one is the inner side of things, the upper one the outer. Each trigram has an image (Heaven, Earth, Thunder, Water, Mountain, Wind, Fire or Lake), which is why the cards say things like "Water over Thunder."
